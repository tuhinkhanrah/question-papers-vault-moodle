  <generalfeedback format="html"><text><![CDATA[<p><strong>Step 1: Evaluation of Assertion</strong><br/>Although electron gain enthalpy generally tends to become less negative down a group, this trend does not hold unconditionally throughout the periodic table. Specifically, in Groups 16 and 17, the second-period elements (oxygen and fluorine) have exceptionally small atomic sizes, causing strong interelectronic repulsions in their compact \(2p\) subshells. As a result, the electron gain enthalpy of chlorine (\(-349\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}\)) is more negative than that of fluorine (\(-328\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}\)), and that of sulfur (\(-200\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}\)) is more negative than that of oxygen (\(-141\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}\)). Because electron gain enthalpy becomes more negative rather than less negative when moving from the second to third period, the blanket statement that electron gain enthalpy becomes less negative as we go down a group is incorrect.</p><p><strong>Step 2: Evaluation of Reason</strong><br/>On descending any group in the periodic table, extra electron shells are progressively added, which increases the atomic radius and places the valence shell farther from the nucleus. This physical description is entirely correct.</p><p><strong>Step 3: Final Conclusion</strong><br/>The assertion is an incorrect statement, whereas the reason is a correct statement.</p>]]></text></generalfeedback>
