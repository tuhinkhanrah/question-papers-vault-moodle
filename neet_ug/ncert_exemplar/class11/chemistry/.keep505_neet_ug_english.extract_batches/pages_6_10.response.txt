  <questiontext format="html"><text><![CDATA[
<p><strong>Assertion (A):</strong> Three states of matter are the result of balance between intermolecular forces and thermal energy of the molecules.</p>
<p><strong>Reason (R):</strong> Intermolecular forces tend to keep the molecules together but thermal energy of molecules tends to keep them apart.</p>
]]></text></questiontext>
  <generalfeedback format="html"><text><![CDATA[
<p><strong>Step 1: Conceptual Principle</strong><br/>The physical state of a substance (solid, liquid, or gas) depends upon the competition between intermolecular attractive forces and the thermal energy of the constituent particles.</p>
<p><strong>Step 2: Evaluation of Assertion and Reason</strong><br/>Intermolecular forces are cohesive forces that tend to draw molecules together and hold them in fixed or condensed arrangements. Conversely, thermal energy arises from molecular motion and tends to keep particles separated by causing them to move randomly and rapidly. When intermolecular forces dominate, solids or liquids form; when thermal energy dominates, a gas forms. Therefore, the observed physical state is determined by the mutual balance between these two opposing factors.</p>
<p><strong>Step 3: Final Conclusion</strong><br/>Both Assertion and Reason are scientifically accurate statements, and Reason serves as the correct explanation for Assertion.</p>

  <name><text>NEET_UG_CHEMISTRY_STATESOFMATTER_ASSERTIONREASON_Q45 - Product of pressure and volume for real gases</text></name>
  <questiontext format="html"><text><![CDATA[
<p><strong>Assertion (A):</strong> At constant temperature, \(pV\) vs \(V\) plot for real gases is not a straight line.</p>
<p><strong>Reason (R):</strong> At high pressure all gases have \(Z > 1\) but at intermediate pressure most gases have \(Z < 1\).</p>
]]></text></questiontext>
  <generalfeedback format="html"><text><![CDATA[
<p><strong>Step 1: Conceptual Principle</strong><br/>For an ideal gas at constant temperature, the product \(pV\) is invariant with volume or pressure, producing a horizontal straight line. For real gases, deviations from ideal behaviour are quantified by the compressibility factor \(Z = \frac{pV}{nRT}\).</p>
<p><strong>Step 2: Evaluation of Assertion and Reason</strong><br/>At intermediate pressures, attractive intermolecular forces dominate, drawing molecules together and making the molar volume smaller than ideal, so that \(Z < 1\) and \(pV\) decreases below the ideal value. At high pressures, repulsive forces and the finite molecular volume become significant, making compression difficult, so that \(Z > 1\) and \(pV\) rises well above the ideal value. Because \(Z\) continuously changes with pressure and volume across these regimes, the product \(pV\) is not constant, and its plot versus \(V\) is curved rather than a straight line.</p>
<p><strong>Step 3: Final Conclusion</strong><br/>Both Assertion and Reason are true statements, and Reason correctly explains why the plot is not a straight line.</p>
]]></text></generalfeedback>

Hence, a gas cannot be liquefied at temperatures exceeding its critical temperature.</p>

  <name><text>NEET_UG_CHEMISTRY_STATESOFMATTER_ASSERTIONREASON_Q49 - Surface molecules and spherical shape of liquid drops</text></name>
  <questiontext format="html"><text><![CDATA[
<p><strong>Assertion (A):</strong> Liquids tend to have maximum number of molecules at their surface.</p>
<p><strong>Reason (R):</strong> Small liquid drops have spherical shape.</p>
]]></text></questiontext>
  <generalfeedback format="html"><text><![CDATA[
<p><strong>Step 1: Conceptual Principle</strong><br/>Molecules located at the surface of a liquid experience an unbalanced inward net attractive force towards the bulk liquid. Consequently, surface molecules possess higher potential energy than bulk molecules.</p>
<p><strong>Step 2: Evaluation of Assertion and Reason</strong><br/>To reach a state of minimum energy and maximum stability, liquids tend to minimize their surface area, meaning they tend to have the minimum possible number of molecules at their surface, not the maximum. Therefore, Assertion is incorrect. On the other hand, for a given volume, a sphere provides the minimum surface area, which explains why small liquid drops assume a spherical shape due to surface tension. Thus, Reason is correct.</p>
<p><strong>Step 3: Final Conclusion</strong><br/>Assertion is incorrect, but Reason is correct.</p>
]]></text></generalfeedback>
