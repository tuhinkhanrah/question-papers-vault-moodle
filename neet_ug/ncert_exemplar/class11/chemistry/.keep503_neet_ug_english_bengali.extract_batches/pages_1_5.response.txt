<p><strong>Step 2: Evaluation of Given Species</strong><br/>The given species each have 10 electrons. Consequently, a cation with a higher positive charge has a smaller radius than a cation with a lower positive charge, while an anion with a higher negative charge has a larger radius due to greater electron-electron repulsion relative to nuclear pull.</p>
<p><strong>Step 3: Final Conclusion</strong><br/>Arranging the species in order of increasing ionic radius yields \(\text{Mg}^{2+} < \text{Na}^+ < \text{F}^- < \text{O}^{2-}\).</p>

  <generalfeedback format="html"><text><![CDATA[<p><strong>Step 1: Conceptual Principle</strong><br/>The screening (shielding) effect represents the reduction in effective nuclear charge felt by outer-shell electrons due to the electron density of inner-shell orbitals. The extent of shielding depends on the penetration power and spatial shape of the orbitals of a given principal energy level.</p>
<p><strong>Step 2: Evaluation of Orbital Shapes and Penetration</strong><br/>Orbitals with higher penetration power spend more time closer to the nucleus and possess higher radial electron density near the nucleus. An \(\text{s}\)-orbital is spherical and penetrates closest to the nucleus, thus shielding outer electrons most effectively. The \(\text{p}\)-orbitals have directional lobes with intermediate penetration, while \(\text{d}\) and \(\text{f}\) orbitals have larger, highly diffused shapes that provide poor and ineffective shielding.</p>

<question type="multichoice">
  <name><text>NEET_UG_CHEMISTRY_2025_CLASSIFICATION_OF_ELEMENTS_AND_PERIODICITY_IN_PROPERTIES_MCQ_Q04 - Order of first ionisation enthalpies of elements</text></name>
  <questiontext format="html"><text><![CDATA[<p>The first ionisation enthalpies of \(\text{Na}\), \(\text{Mg}\), \(\text{Al}\), and \(\text{Si}\) are in the order:</p>
<hr/>
<p>\(\text{Na}\), \(\text{Mg}\), \(\text{Al}\), এবং \(\text{Si}\)-এর প্রথম আয়নন এনথ্যালপির ক্রম হলো:</p>]]></text></questiontext>
  <generalfeedback format="html"><text><![CDATA[<p><strong>Step 1: Conceptual Principle</strong><br/>First ionisation enthalpy is the energy required to remove the most loosely bound electron from an isolated neutral gaseous atom. Across a period from left to right, ionisation enthalpy generally increases due to increasing effective nuclear charge. However, completely filled or half-filled subshells possess extra stability, leading to anomalies.</p>
<p><strong>Step 2: Electronic Configuration and Evaluation</strong><br/>The valence shell configurations of the third period elements are:<br/>\(\text{Na}\) (\(Z = 11\)): \([\text{Ne}]\,3\text{s}^1\)<br/>\(\text{Mg}\) (\(Z = 12\)): \([\text{Ne}]\,3\text{s}^2\)<br/>\(\text{Al}\) (\(Z = 13\)): \([\text{Ne}]\,3\text{s}^2\,3\text{p}^1\)<br/>\(\text{Si}\) (\(Z = 14\)): \([\text{Ne}]\,3\text{s}^2\,3\text{p}^2\)<br/>Removing an electron from magnesium requires removing a \(3\text{s}\) electron from a completely filled, stable \(3\text{s}^2\) subshell. In aluminum, the electron to be removed is in a higher-energy \(3\text{p}\) subshell, which is shielded by the inner \(3\text{s}^2\) electrons and held less firmly. Therefore, the first ionisation enthalpy of magnesium is greater than that of aluminum (\(\text{Mg} > \text{Al}\)). Along the period, silicon has a higher nuclear charge and smaller atomic radius than aluminum, so silicon has a higher ionisation enthalpy than aluminum (\(\text{Al} < \text{Si}\)). Sodium has only one valence electron in \(3\text{s}\) with the lowest nuclear charge among the four, giving it the lowest ionisation enthalpy.</p>
<p><strong>Step 3: Final Conclusion</strong><br/>The actual values are \(\text{Na}\) (\(496\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}\)), \(\text{Mg}\) (\(737\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}\)), \(\text{Al}\) (\(577\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}\)), and \(\text{Si}\) (\(786\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}\)). Thus, the order is \(\text{Na} < \text{Mg} > \text{Al} < \text{Si}\).</p>

  <questiontext format="html"><text><![CDATA[<p>Among halogens, the correct order of amount of energy released in electron gain (electron gain enthalpy) is:</p>

  <generalfeedback format="html"><text><![CDATA[<p><strong>Step 1: Conceptual Principle</strong><br/>Electron gain enthalpy is the enthalpy change associated with the addition of an electron to an isolated neutral gaseous atom. The amount of energy released corresponds to the magnitude of the negative electron gain enthalpy. Down group 17, as atomic size increases, electron gain enthalpy generally becomes less negative because the incoming electron experiences weaker nuclear attraction.</p>
<p><strong>Step 2: Anomaly Between Fluorine and Chlorine</strong><br/>Fluorine has an exceptionally compact atomic size with its valence electrons situated in a small \(2\text{p}\) subshell. When an additional electron is added to fluorine, it experiences substantial interelectronic repulsion from the existing electrons in the tight \(2\text{p}\) orbital. In chlorine, the incoming electron enters the larger, more spacious \(3\text{p}\) subshell where interelectronic repulsion is significantly lower. Consequently, more energy is released when chlorine gains an electron than when fluorine gains one.</p>
<p><strong>Step 3: Final Conclusion</strong><br/>The negative electron gain enthalpy values (in \(\text{kJ mol}^{-1}\)) are \(\text{Cl}\) (\(-349\)), \(\text{F}\) (\(-328\)), \(\text{Br}\) (\(-325\)), and \(\text{I}\) (\(-295\)). Thus, the order of magnitude of energy released is \(\text{Cl} > \text{F} > \text{Br} > \text{I}\), which corresponds to the relationship \(\text{F} < \text{Cl} > \text{Br} > \text{I}\).</p>

  <generalfeedback format="html"><text><![CDATA[<p><strong>Step 1: Conceptual Principle</strong><br/>Periodic trends are governed by atomic structure, effective nuclear charge, and subshell stability. Anomalies arise when subshells are half-filled or when electrons experience interelectronic repulsions in compact orbitals.</p>
<p><strong>Step 2: Detailed Evaluation of Each Arrangement</strong><br/>1. \(\text{Al}^{3+} < \text{Mg}^{2+} < \text{Na}^+ < \text{F}^-\) (increasing ionic size): These are isoelectronic ions (10 electrons each). As the nuclear charge decreases from \(\text{Al}\) (\(Z = 13\)) to \(\text{F}\) (\(Z = 9\)), the ionic radius increases. This order correctly agrees with the property.<br/>2. \(\text{B} < \text{C} < \text{N} < \text{O}\) (increasing first ionisation enthalpy): Nitrogen has a stable half-filled \(2\text{p}^3\) subshell (\(1402\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}\)), making its first ionisation enthalpy higher than that of oxygen (\(1314\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}\)), where removing an electron relieves electron pairing in \(2\text{p}^4\). The correct order is \(\text{B} < \text{C} < \text{O} < \text{N}\). Hence, the given order does not agree.<br/>3. \(\text{I} < \text{Br} < \text{Cl} < \text{F}\) (increasing electron gain enthalpy): Chlorine has the highest negative electron gain enthalpy due to less interelectronic repulsion in the \(3\text{p}\) orbital compared to the small \(2\text{p}\) orbital of fluorine. The correct order of magnitude is \(\text{I} < \text{Br} < \text{F} < \text{Cl}\). Hence, the given order does not agree.<br/>4. \(\text{Li} < \text{Na} < \text{K} < \text{Rb}\) (increasing metallic radius): Down group 1, principal quantum number increases, adding new electronic shells and expanding the metallic radius. This order correctly agrees with the property.</p>
<p><strong>Step 3: Final Conclusion</strong><br/>The orders that do not agree with the indicated variation of property are \(\text{B} < \text{C} < \text{N} < \text{O}\) for first ionisation enthalpy and \(\text{I} < \text{Br} < \text{Cl} < \text{F}\) for electron gain enthalpy.</p>

  <generalfeedback format="html"><text><![CDATA[<p><strong>Step 1: Conceptual Principle</strong><br/>Physical quantities that represent energies or enthalpy changes have dimensions of energy per amount of substance, typically expressed in \(\text{kJ mol}^{-1}\) or \(\text{eV atom}^{-1}\). Relative scales or qualitative periodic tendencies are dimensionless and have no units.</p>
<p><strong>Step 2: Evaluation of Options</strong><br/>1. Electronegativity is a relative measure of the tendency of an atom in a chemical molecule to attract shared electrons towards itself. Scales such as the Pauling scale, Mulliken-Jaffe scale, and Allred-Rochow scale assign arbitrary, dimensionless relative numbers (e.g., fluorine is 4.0 on the Pauling scale). It has no units.<br/>2. Electron gain enthalpy is a thermodynamic enthalpy change, typically measured and expressed in units of \(\text{kJ mol}^{-1}\).<br/>3. Ionisation enthalpy is the quantity of energy required to remove an electron, also measured and expressed in units of \(\text{kJ mol}^{-1}\).<br/>4. Metallic character describes the relative qualitative tendency of an atom to lose valence electrons and behave electropositively. It is a descriptive periodic property and does not possess a physical unit.</p>
<p><strong>Step 3: Final Conclusion</strong><br/>Electronegativity and metallic character have no units.</p>
