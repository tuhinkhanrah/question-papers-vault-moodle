  </questiontext>
  <generalfeedback format="html">
    <text><![CDATA[
      <p><strong>Step 1: Conceptual Principle</strong><br/>
      Translatory motion is characterized by every particle of a rigid body shifting along parallel trajectories such that any line segment connecting two points in the body remains parallel to its initial orientation at all times.</p>
      <p><strong>Step 2: Condition for Uniformity</strong><br/>
      For translatory motion to be uniform, the velocity of the body must remain constant in both magnitude and direction over time. This requires the net acceleration of the object to be zero.</p>
      <p><strong>Step 3: Rotational Exclusion</strong><br/>
      If the body were spinning about an axis or rotating about its centre of mass, different points on the body would possess different tangential velocities. Pure translatory motion precludes any rotation or spin, thereby ensuring that all constituent particles of the object possess identical linear velocity vectors at every instant.</p>
      <p><strong>Step 4: Check and Final Answer</strong><br/>
      A state of rest is merely a special static case with zero speed, whereas uniform translatory motion permits non-zero constant velocity. A circular path requires centripetal acceleration, which constantly changes velocity direction. Thus, uniform translatory motion requires all parts of the ball to share an identical, constant velocity vector.<br/>
      Final conceptual result: all parts of the ball have the same velocity (magnitude and direction) and the velocity is constant.</p>

      <p><strong>Step 2: Linear Equilibrium</strong><br/>
      According to Newton's second law for a rigid body, the net external force is proportional to the linear acceleration of its centre of mass:
      \[\mathbf{F}_{\text{net}} = m\frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt}\]

This would introduce rotation, causing the linear velocities of different points along the scale to vary and change continuously with time. Thus, to maintain uniform velocity without rotation, the net torque acting about the centre of mass must also be zero.</p>
      <p><strong>Step 4: Check and Final Answer</strong><br/>
      Both translational equilibrium and rotational equilibrium must simultaneously hold for an extended body moving with steady linear velocity.<br/>
      Final conceptual result: the force acting on the scale is zero and the torque acting about centre of mass of the scale is also zero.</p>

  <name><text>NEET_UG_PHYSICS_MCQI_LAWS_OF_MOTION_MCQ_Q05 - Derivation of momentum conservation from Newtons laws</text></name>
  <questiontext format="html">
    <text><![CDATA[<p>Conservation of momentum in a collision between particles can be understood from</p>]]></text>
  </questiontext>
  <generalfeedback format="html">
    <text><![CDATA[
      <p><strong>Step 1: Conceptual Principle</strong><br/>
      Consider two particles colliding during a short time interval \(\Delta t\). Let \(\mathbf{F}_{12}\) be the force exerted on particle 1 by particle 2, and \(\mathbf{F}_{21}\) be the force exerted on particle 2 by particle 1.</p>
      <p><strong>Step 2: Role of Newton's Second Law</strong><br/>
      Newton's second law relates the force on each particle to its rate of change of momentum:
      \[\Delta \mathbf{p}_1 = \mathbf{F}_{12}\Delta t \quad \text{and} \quad \Delta \mathbf{p}_2 = \mathbf{F}_{21}\Delta t\]</p>
      <p><strong>Step 3: Role of Newton's Third Law</strong><br/>
      Newton's third law states that mutual forces between two bodies are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction:
      \[\mathbf{F}_{12} = -\mathbf{F}_{21} \implies \mathbf{F}_{12} + \mathbf{F}_{21} = \mathbf{0}\]
      Summing the momentum changes of both interacting bodies gives:
      \[\Delta \mathbf{p}_1 + \Delta \mathbf{p}_2 = (\mathbf{F}_{12} + \mathbf{F}_{21})\Delta t = \mathbf{0}\]
      This demonstrates that the total momentum of the isolated system is conserved.</p>
      <p><strong>Step 4: Conclusion and Final Answer</strong><br/>
      Newton's second law is required to define force in terms of momentum change, and Newton's third law is required to establish that internal collision forces cancel in pairs. Thus, both laws are collectively necessary.<br/>
      Final conceptual result: both Newton's second and third law.</p>

    <text><![CDATA[<p>Two billiard balls A and B, each of mass \(50\,\text{g}\) and moving in opposite directions with speed of \(5\,\text{m s}^{-1}\) each, collide and rebound with the same speed. If the collision lasts for \(10^{-3}\,\text{s}\), which of the following statements are true?</p>]]></text>
  </questiontext>
  <generalfeedback format="html">
    <text><![CDATA[
      <p><strong>Step 1: Given Data and Sign Convention</strong><br/>
      Mass of each ball: \(m = 50\,\text{g} = 0.05\,\text{kg}\).<br/>
      Collision duration: \(\Delta t = 10^{-3}\,\text{s}\).<br/>
      Let the initial velocity of ball A be positive: \(u_A = +5\,\text{m s}^{-1}\), so after rebound \(v_A = -5\,\text{m s}^{-1}\).<br/>
      For ball B moving in the opposite direction: \(u_B = -5\,\text{m s}^{-1}\), so after rebound \(v_B = +5\,\text{m s}^{-1}\).</p>
      <p><strong>Step 2: Calculation of Impulse</strong><br/>
      The impulse imparted to ball A is:
      \[J_A = \Delta p_A = m(v_A - u_A) = 0.05 \times (-5 - 5) = -0.5\,\text{N s} = -0.5\,\text{kg m s}^{-1}\]
      The impulse imparted to ball B is:
      \[J_B = \Delta p_B = m(v_B - u_B) = 0.05 \times (5 - (-5)) = +0.5\,\text{N s} = +0.5\,\text{kg m s}^{-1}\]
      The magnitude of impulse imparted to each ball is \(0.5\,\text{N s}\).</p>
      <p><strong>Step 3: Force Evaluation and Action-Reaction Pair</strong><br/>
      The average force on ball A is:
      \[F_A = \frac{\Delta p_A}{\Delta t} = \frac{-0.5}{10^{-3}} = -500\,\text{N}\]
      The average force on ball B is:
      \[F_B = \frac{\Delta p_B}{\Delta t} = \frac{+0.5}{10^{-3}} = +500\,\text{N}\]
      By Newton's third law, the impulse and the force exerted on ball A and ball B form action-reaction pairs; they are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction.</p>
      <p><strong>Step 4: Check and Final Answer</strong><br/>
      The magnitude of the impulse is \(0.5\,\text{N s}\), and mutual collision forces and impulses between the two bodies are equal in magnitude and directed oppositely.<br/>
      Final conceptual result: The impulse imparted to each ball is \(0.5\,\text{N s}\), and the impulse and the force on each ball are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction.</p>

  <questiontext format="html">
    <text><![CDATA[<p>A body of mass \(10\,\text{kg}\) is acted upon by two perpendicular forces, \(6\,\text{N}\) and \(8\,\text{N}\). The resultant acceleration of the body is</p>]]></text>
  </questiontext>
  <generalfeedback format="html">
    <text><![CDATA[
      <p><strong>Step 1: Given Data and Conditions</strong><br/>
      Mass of the body: \(m = 10\,\text{kg}\).<br/>
      Two mutually perpendicular forces: \(F_1 = 6\,\text{N}\) and \(F_2 = 8\,\text{N}\).</p>
      <p><strong>Step 2: Magnitude of Resultant Force and Acceleration</strong><br/>
      Because the forces are perpendicular, their vector sum has magnitude:
      \[F_{\text{net}} = \sqrt{F_1^2 + F_2^2} = \sqrt{6^2 + 8^2} = \sqrt{36 + 64} = \sqrt{100} = 10\,\text{N}\]
      By Newton's second law, the magnitude of the resultant acceleration is:
      \[a = \frac{F_{\text{net}}}{m} = \frac{10\,\text{N}}{10\,\text{kg}} = 1\,\text{m s}^{-2}\]</p>
      <p><strong>Step 3: Direction of Acceleration</strong><br/>
      Acceleration is parallel to the resultant force vector.<br/>
      Let \(\theta_1\) be the angle made with respect to the \(6\,\text{N}\) force:
      \[\tan \theta_1 = \frac{F_2}{F_1} = \frac{8}{6} = \frac{4}{3} \implies \theta_1 = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{4}{3}\right)\]
      Let \(\theta_2\) be the angle made with respect to the \(8\,\text{N}\) force:
      \[\tan \theta_2 = \frac{F_1}{F_2} = \frac{6}{8} = \frac{3}{4} \implies \theta_2 = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)\]
      Both angular formulations define the identical physical vector in space.</p>
      <p><strong>Step 4: Check and Final Answer</strong><br/>
      The magnitude is strictly \(1\,\text{m s}^{-2}\), which acts at \(\tan^{-1}(4/3)\) with respect to the \(6\,\text{N}\) force and equivalently at \(\tan^{-1}(3/4)\) with respect to the \(8\,\text{N}\) force.<br/>
      Final conceptual result: \(1\,\text{m s}^{-2}\) at an angle of \(\tan^{-1}(4/3)\) with respect to the \(6\,\text{N}\) force, and \(1\,\text{m s}^{-2}\) at an angle of \(\tan^{-1}(3/4)\) with respect to the \(8\,\text{N}\) force.</p>
