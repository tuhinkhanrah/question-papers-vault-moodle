  </name>
  <questiontext format="html">
    <text><![CDATA[<p>Among the four graphs, there is only one graph for which average velocity over the time interval \( (0, T) \) can vanish for a suitably chosen \( T \). Which one is it?</p>]]></text>
  </questiontext>
  <generalfeedback format="html">
    <text><![CDATA[<p><strong>Step 1: Given Data and Conditions</strong><br/>Four position-time (\(x\)-\(t\)) graphs describe one-dimensional motion beginning at \(t = 0\). The objective is to identify the graph for which the average velocity over the time interval \((0, T)\) vanishes for some suitably chosen \(T > 0\).</p><p><strong>Step 2: Assumptions and Sign Conventions</strong><br/>Motion takes place along a straight line with coordinate position \(x\) as a function of time \(t \ge 0\). The average velocity over an interval \((0, T)\) is defined as the net displacement divided by the elapsed time duration.</p><p><strong>Step 3: Relevant Formulae and Principles</strong><br/>Average velocity \(\bar{v}\) over the interval from \(t = 0\) to \(t = T\) is given by:\[ \bar{v} = \frac{x(T) - x(0)}{T} \]For \(\bar{v} = 0\), the numerator must vanish:\[ x(T) - x(0) = 0 \implies x(T) = x(0) \]Geometrically, this condition requires that a horizontal line drawn from the initial position \(x(0)\) at \(t = 0\) intersects the position-time curve again at a later time \(t = T > 0\).</p><p><strong>Step 4: Step-by-Step Working</strong><br/>Analyzing each curve conceptually:<ul><li>In the curve that begins at a negative position, rises monotonically to a peak, and levels off in the positive region, the position never returns to the negative initial value. Hence, \(x(T) \neq x(0)\) for all \(T > 0\).</li><li>In the curve that starts at an initial positive position, rises to a maximum, and subsequently falls below that starting position, a horizontal line drawn through \(x(0)\) crosses the curve again at a later time \(T > 0\). At this intersection, \(x(T) = x(0)\), producing zero net displacement and zero average velocity.</li><li>In the curve that continuously decreases from a positive value toward the time axis, the function is strictly monotonic, so the position never repeats any past value.</li><li>In the curve that starts from the origin and continuously increases, the function is strictly increasing, so the position never returns to the origin.</li></ul></p><p><strong>Step 5: Check and Final Answer</strong><br/>Only the curve that rises from an initial positive value to a maximum and subsequently descends back to cross that same initial value permits \(x(T) = x(0)\) for a finite time \(T > 0\), ensuring the average velocity vanishes.<br/>The correct graph is the one where the position starts at a positive value, peaks, and then decreases back to its initial level.</p>]]></text>

  <generalfeedback format="html">
    <text><![CDATA[<p><strong>Step 1: Given Data and Conditions</strong><br/>A particle executes one-dimensional motion. Its instantaneous speed satisfies the strict bound \(0 \le v < v_0\), where \(v_0 > 0\) is a constant maximum threshold speed.</p><p><strong>Step 2: Assumptions and Sign Conventions</strong><br/>Motion occurs along a straight line coordinate axis \(x\). The instantaneous velocity \(v_x(t)\) can be positive or negative, with its magnitude bounded by \(|v_x(t)| < v_0\).</p><p><strong>Step 3: Relevant Formulae and Principles</strong><br/>The net displacement \(x\) over time duration \(T\) is given by the definite integral of velocity:\[ x = \int_0^T v_x(t)\, dt \]Since \(-v_0 < v_x(t) < v_0\) holds for all \(t \in [0, T]\), the integral satisfies:\[ \int_0^T (-v_0)\, dt < \int_0^T v_x(t)\, dt < \int_0^T v_0\, dt \implies -v_0 T < x < v_0 T \]</p><p><strong>Step 4: Step-by-Step Working</strong><br/>- Maximum possible displacement in the positive direction occurs if the particle travels continuously in that direction, which is strictly less than \(v_0 T\).<br/>- Maximum possible displacement in the negative direction occurs if the particle travels continuously in the opposite direction, which is strictly greater than \(-v_0 T\).<br/>- Therefore, displacement \(x\) must strictly satisfy \(-v_0 T < x < v_0 T\).<br/>- The displacement can be negative if motion is toward the negative axis, so it need not always take non-negative values.<br/>- Acceleration is the time rate of change of velocity, which can be positive, negative, or zero.<br/>- The velocity can change sign from positive to negative or vice versa, meaning turning points can certainly occur.</p><p><strong>Step 5: Check and Final Answer</strong><br/>The strict magnitude bound on speed inherently limits the displacement to lie between \(-v_0 T\) and \(v_0 T\).<br/>The displacement \(x\) in time \(T\) satisfies \(-v_0 T < x < v_0 T\).</p>]]></text>

  </name>
  <questiontext format="html">
    <text><![CDATA[<p>A vehicle travels half the distance \(L\) with speed \(V_1\) and the other half with speed \(V_2\), then its average speed is</p>]]></text>
  </questiontext>
  <generalfeedback format="html">
    <text><![CDATA[<p><strong>Step 1: Given Data and Conditions</strong><br/>A vehicle covers a total distance \(L\). The first half distance \(d_1 = \frac{L}{2}\) is covered at speed \(V_1\), and the second half distance \(d_2 = \frac{L}{2}\) is covered at speed \(V_2\).</p><p><strong>Step 2: Assumptions and Sign Conventions</strong><br/>The speeds \(V_1\) and \(V_2\) are constant scalar speeds over their respective distance intervals.</p><p><strong>Step 3: Relevant Formulae and Principles</strong><br/>Average speed is defined as total distance divided by total time:\[ v_{\text{avg}} = \frac{d_{\text{total}}}{t_{\text{total}}} = \frac{L}{t_1 + t_2} \]where \(t_1 = \frac{d_1}{V_1}\) and \(t_2 = \frac{d_2}{V_2}\).</p><p><strong>Step 4: Step-by-Step Working</strong><br/>- Time taken for the first half:\[ t_1 = \frac{L/2}{V_1} = \frac{L}{2V_1} \]- Time taken for the second half:\[ t_2 = \frac{L/2}{V_2} = \frac{L}{2V_2} \]- Total time taken:\[ t_{\text{total}} = t_1 + t_2 = \frac{L}{2V_1} + \frac{L}{2V_2} = \frac{L}{2}\left(\frac{V_1 + V_2}{V_1 V_2}\right) \]- Average speed:\[ v_{\text{avg}} = \frac{L}{t_{\text{total}}} = \frac{L}{\frac{L(V_1 + V_2)}{2V_1 V_2}} = \frac{2V_1 V_2}{V_1 + V_2} \]</p><p><strong>Step 5: Check and Final Answer</strong><br/>When \(V_1 = V_2 = V\), \(v_{\text{avg}} = \frac{2V^2}{2V} = V\), which confirms physical consistency. The result corresponds to the harmonic mean of the two speeds.<br/>The correct average speed is \(\frac{2V_1 V_2}{V_1 + V_2}\).</p>]]></text>

The interval of interest is from \(t = 0\text{ s}\) to \(t = 4\text{ s}\).</p><p><strong>Step 2: Assumptions and Sign Conventions</strong><br/>Motion takes place in one dimension along the \(x\)-axis. Distance is the total path length traversed, which accumulates regardless of the direction of motion, while displacement is the net difference between final and initial positions.</p><p><strong>Step 3: Relevant Formulae and Principles</strong><br/>- Instantaneous velocity:\[ v(t) = \frac{dx}{dt} \]- A turning point occurs where \(v(t) = 0\).<br/>- Total distance is calculated by summing the absolute path lengths traveled across each monotonic segment between turning points.</p><p><strong>Step 4: Step-by-Step Working</strong><br/>- Differentiating position with respect to time:\[ v(t) = \frac{d}{dt}\left[(t - 2)^2\right] = 2(t - 2)\text{ m/s} \]- Setting velocity to zero to identify turning points:\[ 2(t - 2) = 0 \implies t = 2\text{ s} \]Since \(t = 2\text{ s}\) lies within \([0, 4\text{ s}]\), the particle changes its direction of motion at \(t = 2\text{ s}\).<br/>- Finding positions at boundaries and the turning point:<ul><li>At \(t = 0\text{ s}\): \(x(0) = (0 - 2)^2 = 4\text{ m}\)</li><li>At \(t = 2\text{ s}\): \(x(2) = (2 - 2)^2 = 0\text{ m}\)</li><li>At \(t = 4\text{ s}\): \(x(4) = (4 - 2)^2 = 4\text{ m}\)</li></ul>- Distance traveled during \(t = 0\text{ s}\) to \(t = 2\text{ s}\):\[ s_1 = |x(2) - x(0)| = |0 - 4| = 4\text{ m} \]- Distance traveled during \(t = 2\text{ s}\) to \(t = 4\text{ s}\):\[ s_2 = |x(4) - x(2)| = |4 - 0| = 4\text{ m} \]- Total distance covered in the first 4 seconds:\[ s = s_1 + s_2 = 4\text{ m} + 4\text{ m} = 8\text{ m} \]</p><p><strong>Step 5: Check and Final Answer</strong><br/>The net displacement over 4 seconds is \(x(4) - x(0) = 4 - 4 = 0\text{ m}\), but accounting for the reversal of direction at \(t = 2\text{ s}\) yields a total path length of \(8\text{ m}\).<br/>The distance covered by the particle in the first 4 seconds is \(8\text{ m}\).</p>]]></text>

  </name>
  <questiontext format="html">
    <text><![CDATA[<p>At a metro station, a girl walks up a stationary escalator in time \(t_1\). If she remains stationary on the escalator, then the escalator takes her up in time \(t_2\). The time taken by her to walk up on the moving escalator will be</p>]]></text>
  </questiontext>
  <generalfeedback format="html">
    <text><![CDATA[<p><strong>Step 1: Given Data and Conditions</strong><br/>Let the distance along the escalator be \(L\).<br/>- Time taken by the girl walking up the stationary escalator: \(t_1\)<br/>- Time taken by the escalator moving with the stationary girl: \(t_2\)<br/>We need to determine the time \(t\) taken by the girl when she walks up the moving escalator.</p><p><strong>Step 2: Assumptions and Sign Conventions</strong><br/>Motion is along the straight path of the escalator in the upward direction. Both speeds are constant and reinforce each other.</p><p><strong>Step 3: Relevant Formulae and Principles</strong><br/>- Speed of the girl relative to the escalator:\[ v_{\text{girl}} = \frac{L}{t_1} \]- Speed of the escalator relative to the ground:\[ v_{\text{esc}} = \frac{L}{t_2} \]- When she walks up the moving escalator, the resultant speed relative to the ground is:\[ v = v_{\text{girl}} + v_{\text{esc}} \]- Time taken to travel distance \(L\) at speed \(v\) is:\[ t = \frac{L}{v} \]</p><p><strong>Step 4: Step-by-Step Working</strong><br/>- Adding the two speeds:\[ v = \frac{L}{t_1} + \frac{L}{t_2} = L\left(\frac{1}{t_1} + \frac{1}{t_2}\right) = L\left(\frac{t_1 + t_2}{t_1 t_2}\right) \]- Calculating the time taken:\[ t = \frac{L}{v} = \frac{L}{L\left(\frac{t_1 + t_2}{t_1 t_2}\right)} = \frac{t_1 t_2}{t_1 + t_2} \]</p><p><strong>Step 5: Check and Final Answer</strong><br/>Checking dimensional consistency gives units of time, and the resulting time is strictly less than either \(t_1\) or \(t_2\), which is physically required since both motions assist each other.<br/>The time taken by her to walk up on the moving escalator is \(\frac{t_1 t_2}{t_2 + t_1}\).</p>]]></text>
