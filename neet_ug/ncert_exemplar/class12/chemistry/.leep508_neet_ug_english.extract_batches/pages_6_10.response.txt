The maximum oxidation state of a transition element is determined by the total number of valence electrons present in its outermost \(s\) and \(d\) orbitals that can participate in chemical bonding.</p>
<p><strong>Evaluation:</strong><br/>
- The assertion states that the highest oxidation state of osmium is \(+8\). This is true (for example, in osmium tetroxide, \(\mathrm{OsO}_4\), and osmium octafluoride, \(\mathrm{OsF}_8\)).<br/>
- The reason states that osmium is a \(5d\)-block element. This is factually true, as osmium (\(Z = 76\)) belongs to the third transition (\(5d\)) series.<br/>
- However, being a \(5d\)-block element does not explain why its highest oxidation state is \(+8\). Many other \(5d\)-block elements (such as gold, platinum, or hafnium) are also \(5d\)-block elements but do not show an oxidation state of \(+8\). The true reason osmium shows a \(+8\) oxidation state is that its ground-state valence shell has 8 valence electrons (\(5d^6 6s^2\)), all of which can participate in bonding with strongly electronegative elements like oxygen.<br/>
Therefore, both assertion and reason are true, but reason is not the correct explanation of assertion.</p>
