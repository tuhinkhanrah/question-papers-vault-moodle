<p><strong>Step 2: Evaluation of Each Defect</strong><br/>1. Simple vacancy defect occurs in non-ionic solids when lattice sites are vacant, decreasing the mass per unit volume and hence decreasing the density.<br/>2. Simple interstitial defect occurs in non-ionic solids when extra constituent particles occupy interstitial sites, increasing the mass per unit volume and hence increasing the density.<br/>3. Frenkel defect occurs in ionic solids when a smaller ion is dislocated from its normal site to an interstitial site; since no ion leaves the crystal, the density remains the same.<br/>4. Schottky defect occurs in ionic solids when equal numbers of cations and anions are missing from their lattice sites, decreasing the density.</p>
<p><strong>Step 3: Final Conclusion</strong><br/>Thus, simple vacancy defect matches non-ionic solids with decreased density; simple interstitial defect matches non-ionic solids with increased density; Frenkel defect matches ionic solids with unchanged density; and Schottky defect matches ionic solids with decreased density.</p>

In addition to the 1 atom from the corners, there are 6 face-centred atoms contributing \(6 \times \frac{1}{2} = 3\) atoms.<br/>4. End-centred orthorhombic unit cell: In an orthorhombic system, the three perpendicular edges are unequal (\(a \neq b \neq c\)). In addition to the 1 atom from the corners, there are 2 atoms at the centres of two opposite faces contributing \(2 \times \frac{1}{2} = 1\) atom.</p>
<p><strong>Step 3: Final Conclusion</strong><br/>Each unit cell type matches its corresponding edge-length condition and additional atomic contribution as analysed above.</p>

  <name><text>NEET_UG_CHEMISTRY_MATCHING_SOLIDSTATE_MATCHING_Q67 - Matching conductors and doped semiconductors</text></name>
  <questiontext format="html"><text><![CDATA[<p>Match the items given in Column I with the items given in Column II.</p><hr/><p>स्तंभ I में दी गई मदों का स्तंभ II में दी गई मदों से मिलान कीजिए।</p>]]></text></questiontext>
  <generalfeedback format="html"><text><![CDATA[<p><strong>Step 1: Conceptual Principle</strong><br/>Materials conduct electricity either through free electrons (electronic conductors), mobile ions (electrolytic conductors), or doped semiconductor charge carriers (\(n\)-type via extra electrons, \(p\)-type via holes).</p>
<p><strong>Step 2: Evaluation</strong><br/>1. \(\mathrm{Mg}\) in the solid state is a metal that conducts electricity through mobile valence electrons, making it an electronic conductor.<br/>2. \(\mathrm{MgCl_2}\) in the molten state contains free mobile \(\mathrm{Mg}^{2+}\) and \(\mathrm{Cl}^-\) ions, making it an electrolytic conductor.<br/>3. Silicon (Group 14) doped with phosphorus (Group 15) has extra delocalised electrons, forming an \(n\)-type semiconductor.<br/>4. Germanium (Group 14) doped with boron (Group 13) has electron-deficient holes, forming a \(p\)-type semiconductor.</p>
<p><strong>Step 3: Final Conclusion</strong><br/>Therefore, solid \(\mathrm{Mg}\) matches electronic conductors; molten \(\mathrm{MgCl_2}\) matches electrolytic conductors; silicon with phosphorus matches \(n\)-type semiconductor; and germanium with boron matches \(p\)-type semiconductor.</p>
<hr/>
