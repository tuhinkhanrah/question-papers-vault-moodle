In an ideal toroid with closely spaced turns, the magnetic field is strictly confined to the interior of the toroid and is identically zero everywhere in the open space outside the toroid.</p><p><strong>Step 2: Dipole Field Analysis</strong><br/>If a system has a non-zero net magnetic dipole moment \(\mathbf{m}\), then at large distances \(r \gg R\), its magnetic field must asymptotically approach that of a magnetic dipole, which falls off with distance as \(\frac{1}{r^3}\). However, the magnetic field of an ideal toroid is strictly zero everywhere outside. Hence, the net magnetic dipole moment must be zero.</p><p><strong>Step 3: Symmetry and Final Conclusion</strong><br/>From symmetry, for every individual planar current loop of the winding, there is a diametrically opposite loop carrying current in the opposite direction, so their vector magnetic moments cancel pairwise to yield a net magnetic moment of zero. Therefore, the magnetic moment is zero, otherwise there would be a field falling as \(\frac{1}{r^3}\) at large distances outside the toroid.</p>]]></text></generalfeedback>

  <generalfeedback format="html"><text><![CDATA[<p><strong>Step 1: Domain Theory of Ferromagnetism</strong><br/>A permanent magnet is composed of a ferromagnetic material. In ferromagnetic substances below the Curie temperature, strong exchange coupling between adjacent atomic magnetic moments results in spontaneous magnetisation within microscopic regions called domains. Within each domain, the molecular magnetic moments are aligned in a common direction, giving each domain a net magnetic moment.</p><p><strong>Step 2: Effect of Room Temperature</strong><br/>During the magnetisation process to form a permanent magnet, an external magnetic field causes domains oriented favorably to grow and rotate toward the field direction. After the external field is removed, the domains remain largely aligned due to high retentivity and coercivity. However, at room temperature, thermal agitation and microscopic crystal imperfections prevent complete or perfect alignment of all domains. Perfect alignment occurs only under strong saturating fields or near absolute zero.</p><p><strong>Step 3: Conclusion</strong><br/>Thus, in a permanent magnet at room temperature, the domains are partially aligned, maintaining a strong remanent magnetisation without achieving complete saturation.</p>]]></text></generalfeedback>

  <name><text>NEET_UG_PHYSICS_MAGNETISMANDMATTER_MCQ_Q05 - Magnetisation of paramagnetic sample under changing field</text></name>
  <questiontext format="html"><text><![CDATA[<p>A paramagnetic sample shows a net magnetisation of \(8\text{ A m}^{-1}\) when placed in an external magnetic field of \(0.6\text{ T}\) at a temperature of \(4\text{ K}\). When the same sample is placed in an external magnetic field of \(0.2\text{ T}\) at a temperature of \(16\text{ K}\), the magnetisation will be</p>]]></text></questiontext>
  <generalfeedback format="html"><text><![CDATA[<p><strong>Step 1: Given Data and Conditions</strong><br/>Initial magnetisation of the sample: \(M_1 = 8\text{ A m}^{-1}\)<br/>Initial applied magnetic field: \(B_1 = 0.6\text{ T}\)<br/>Initial absolute temperature: \(T_1 = 4\text{ K}\)<br/>New applied magnetic field: \(B_2 = 0.2\text{ T}\)<br/>New absolute temperature: \(T_2 = 16\text{ K}\)</p><p><strong>Step 2: Assumptions and Physical Model</strong><br/>The sample is paramagnetic and operates far below magnetic saturation, so Curie's law of paramagnetism is applicable.</p><p><strong>Step 3: Relevant Formulae and Principles</strong><br/>According to Curie's law, the magnetisation \(M\) is directly proportional to the applied magnetic field \(B\) and inversely proportional to the absolute temperature \(T\):
\[M \propto \frac{B}{T} \implies \frac{M_2}{M_1} = \left(\frac{B_2}{B_1}\right) \left(\frac{T_1}{T_2}\right)\]</p><p><strong>Step 4: Step-by-Step Working</strong><br/>Substitute the given values into the ratio relation:
\[\frac{M_2}{8\text{ A m}^{-1}} = \left(\frac{0.2\text{ T}}{0.6\text{ T}}\right) \left(\frac{4\text{ K}}{16\text{ K}}\right)\]
Simplify the individual fractions:
\[\frac{0.2}{0.6} = \frac{1}{3}\]
\[\frac{4}{16} = \frac{1}{4}\]
Multiply the fractions:
\[\frac{M_2}{8\text{ A m}^{-1}} = \frac{1}{3} \times \frac{1}{4} = \frac{1}{12}\]
Solve for \(M_2\):
\[M_2 = 8 \times \frac{1}{12}\text{ A m}^{-1} = \frac{2}{3}\text{ A m}^{-1}\]</p><p><strong>Step 5: Check and Final Answer</strong><br/>The magnetic field is reduced by a factor of \(3\) and the absolute temperature is increased by a factor of \(4\). Both changes decrease the alignment of magnetic dipoles, resulting in an overall reduction factor of \(3 \times 4 = 12\). Dividing \(8\text{ A m}^{-1}\) by \(12\) gives \(\frac{2}{3}\text{ A m}^{-1}\), which confirms the result. Thus, the new magnetisation will be \(\frac{2}{3}\text{ A m}^{-1}\).</p>]]></text></generalfeedback>
