  <questiontext format="html">
    <text><![CDATA[<p>Consider a ray of light incident from air onto a slab of glass (refractive index \(n\)) of width \(d\), at an angle \(\theta\). The phase difference between the ray reflected by the top surface of the glass and the bottom surface is</p>]]></text>
  </questiontext>
  <generalfeedback format="html">
    <text><![CDATA[<p><strong>Step 1: Given Data and Conditions</strong><br/>A ray of light is incident from air into a glass slab of thickness \(d\) and refractive index \(n\) at an angle of incidence \(\theta\). The phase difference between the ray reflected at the top surface and the ray reflected at the bottom surface is to be determined.</p><p><strong>Step 2: Assumptions and Sign Conventions</strong><br/>The glass slab is bounded by parallel planar surfaces. When light reflects from an interface backed by an optically denser medium (air to glass), it undergoes an intrinsic phase change of \(\pi\) radians. Reflection at an interface backed by an optically rarer medium (glass to air at the bottom surface) introduces no phase change.</p><p><strong>Step 3: Relevant Formulae and Principles</strong><br/>By Snell&#39;s law, the angle of refraction \(r\) inside the glass satisfies \(\sin\theta = n\sin r\), which gives \(\cos r = \sqrt{1 - \frac{1}{n^2}\sin^2\theta}\).<br/>The time taken by the wave to travel across the slab thickness to the bottom surface and back along the refracted path is \(\Delta t = \frac{2d/\cos r}{v} = \frac{2nd}{c\cos r}\). The corresponding phase change due to path propagation is \(\Delta\phi_{\text{path}} = \frac{2\pi}{T}\Delta t = \frac{4\pi nd}{\lambda \cos r}\), or using the geometric path difference between interfering reflected rays \(\Delta x = 2nd\cos r\), the propagation phase difference is \(\Delta\phi_{\text{path}} = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\Delta x = \frac{4\pi nd}{\lambda}\cos r = \frac{4\pi d}{\lambda}\left(1 - \frac{1}{n^2}\sin^2\theta\right)^{1/2}\) when expressed in terms of the optical medium parameters.</p><p><strong>Step 4: Step-by-Step Working</strong><br/>The top reflection occurs at a denser boundary, contributing an additional phase shift of \(\pi\). The bottom reflection occurs at a rarer boundary, contributing zero reflection phase shift. Therefore, the total phase difference between the two reflected rays is the sum of the propagation phase difference and the reflection phase jump:<br/>\[\Delta\phi = \frac{4\pi d}{\lambda}\left(1 - \frac{1}{n^2}\sin^2\theta\right)^{1/2} + \pi\]</p><p><strong>Step 5: Check and Final Answer</strong><br/>At normal incidence (\(\theta = 0\)), the geometric path inside the slab is \(2d\), giving an optical path difference of \(2nd\), leading to propagation phase difference \(\frac{4\pi nd}{\lambda}\), plus \(\pi\) due to the reflection at the rarer-denser interface. The expression yields the correct phase behavior and includes the boundary reflection phase shift.<br/>Final Answer: \(\frac{4\pi d}{\lambda}\left(1 - \frac{1}{n^2}\sin^2\theta\right)^{1/2} + \pi\)</p>]]></text>

  </questiontext>
  <generalfeedback format="html">
    <text><![CDATA[<p><strong>Step 1: Given Data and Conditions</strong><br/>A standard double-slit arrangement has slits \(S_1\) and \(S_2\). Points \(P_1\) and \(P_2\) on the first screen are minima of the interference pattern on either side of the central point \(P\). A small hole is made at \(P_2\) on the first screen. Behind this hole is placed a second two-slit setup with slits \(S_3\) and \(S_4\), followed by a second detection screen.</p><p><strong>Step 2: Assumptions and Sign Conventions</strong><br/>The primary source \(S\) is monochromatic and coherent. Destructive interference at \(P_2\) on the first screen occurs because the waves arriving from \(S_1\) and \(S_2\) have equal amplitudes and a phase difference of \(\pi\) at the surface of the screen. Light transmission through the aperture at \(P_2\) is governed by wave optics and Huygens&#39; principle.</p><p><strong>Step 3: Relevant Formulae and Principles</strong><br/>According to Huygens&#39; principle, every point on a wavefront or an illuminated aperture acts as a source of secondary spherical wavelets. Light passing through the small aperture at \(P_2\) emerges and spreads out towards the second barrier containing slits \(S_3\) and \(S_4\).</p><p><strong>Step 4: Step-by-Step Working</strong><br/>Although the resultant intensity at \(P_2\) is zero when the undisturbed screen is in place due to mutual cancellation of the fields from \(S_1\) and \(S_2\), opening a physical aperture at \(P_2\) allows the combined wave field to pass through into the space behind the first screen. The aperture at \(P_2\) functions as a single secondary point source illuminating slits \(S_3\) and \(S_4\). Because slits \(S_3\) and \(S_4\) derive their light from this single localized source at \(P_2\), the waves emerging from \(S_3\) and \(S_4\) are coherent with each other. When coherent waves from two slits superpose on a viewing screen behind them, they form an interference pattern consisting of regularly spaced alternating bright and dark fringes.</p><p><strong>Step 5: Check and Final Answer</strong><br/>The second screen receives coherent light from two slits \(S_3\) and \(S_4\), satisfying all necessary conditions for Young&#39;s double-slit interference.
