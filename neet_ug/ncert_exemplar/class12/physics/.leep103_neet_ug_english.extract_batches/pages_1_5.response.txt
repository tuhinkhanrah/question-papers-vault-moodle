However, conservation of charge and the absence of charge accumulation at a junction directly yield Kirchhoff's junction rule.</p>

    \[I = \frac{V}{r + R_{AB}}\]
    The potential drop across AB is:
    \[V_{AB} = I R_{AB} = \frac{V R_{AB}}{r + R_{AB}} = \frac{V}{1 + \frac{r}{R_{AB}}}\]
    The current through \(R'\) is \(I' = \frac{V_{AB}}{R'}\).</p>
    <p><strong>Step 4: Step-by-Step Working</strong><br/>Since \(R' \ge R_0 \gg R\), the ratio \(\frac{R}{R'} \ll 1\), so \(R_{AB} \approx R\) remains nearly constant and is always less than or equal to \(R\) (reaching \(R\) as \(R' \to \infty\)).
    1. Because \(r \ll R \approx R_{AB}\), \(\frac{r}{R_{AB}} \ll 1\), so \(V_{AB} \approx V\) remains nearly constant as \(R'\) is varied.
    2. The current through \(R'\) is \(I' = \frac{V_{AB}}{R'} \approx \frac{V}{R'}\), which varies from \(\frac{V}{R_0}\) down to \(0\) as \(R' \to \infty\), so it is not constant.
    3. The total current \(I = \frac{V}{r + R_{AB}} \approx \frac{V}{r + R}\) does not depend sensitively on \(R'\).
    4. Since \(R_{AB} = \frac{R R'}{R + R'} \le R\) for all \(R' \in [R_0, \infty)\), we have \(r + R_{AB} \le r + R\), which guarantees \(I = \frac{V}{r + R_{AB}} \ge \frac{V}{r + R}\) always.</p>
    <p><strong>Step 5: Check and Final Answer</strong><br/>At the limit \(R' \to \infty\), \(R_{AB} \to R\) and \(I \to \frac{V}{r+R}\). Thus, the potential drop across AB is nearly constant as \(R'\) is varied, and \(I \ge \frac{V}{r + R}\) always.</p>

    \[\rho = \frac{m}{n e^2 \tau}\]
    where \(m\) is the mass of the charge carrier, \(e\) is the magnitude of charge on each carrier, \(n\) is the number density of charge carriers, and \(\tau\) is the relaxation time (average time interval between two successive collisions).</p>

In semiconductors and insulators, \(n\) increases exponentially with temperature \(T\), dominating over the decrease in \(\tau\) and causing \(\rho(T)\) to decrease.</p>
    <p><strong>Step 4: Final Conclusion</strong><br/>Therefore, the temperature dependence of resistivity \(\rho(T)\) is significantly based on the fact that the number of charge carriers can change with temperature \(T\) and the time interval between two successive collisions can depend on \(T\).</p>

  <questiontext format="html"><text><![CDATA[<p>The measurement of an unknown resistance \(R\) is to be carried out using Wheatstone's bridge (see Fig. 3.25 of NCERT Book). Two students perform an experiment in two ways. The first student takes \(R_2 = 10\ \Omega\) and \(R_1 = 5\ \Omega\). The other student takes \(R_2 = 1000\ \Omega\) and \(R_1 = 500\ \Omega\). In the standard arm, both take \(R_3 = 5\ \Omega\).</p><p>Both find \(R = \frac{R_2}{R_1} R_3 = 10\ \Omega\) within errors.</p>]]></text></questiontext>
  <generalfeedback format="html"><text><![CDATA[
    <p><strong>Step 1: Conceptual Principle</strong><br/>In a balanced Wheatstone bridge, the unknown resistance is determined by \(R = \frac{R_2}{R_1} R_3\). The accuracy of this measurement depends on both the precision of the known resistances \(R_1, R_2, R_3\) and the sensitivity of the galvanometer in detecting the null point.</p>
    <p><strong>Step 2: Evaluation</strong><br/>By propagation of errors, the fractional error in \(R\) is:
    \[\frac{\Delta R}{R} = \frac{\Delta R_1}{R_1} + \frac{\Delta R_2}{R_2} + \frac{\Delta R_3}{R_3}\]
    Thus, the errors of measurement directly depend on the accuracy with which \(R_2\) and \(R_1\) can be measured. Any real measurement instrument has finite errors, so the Wheatstone bridge is not completely free of measurement errors.</p>
    <p><strong>Step 3: Application</strong><br/>Comparing the two setups, the second student uses very large resistances (\(R_2 = 1000\ \Omega\) and \(R_1 = 500\ \Omega\)) compared to the first student (\(10\ \Omega\) and \(5\ \Omega\)). Because of the much higher resistance in that branch, the currents through the arms become very feeble. When the current is feeble, a small deviation from the balance condition produces an even smaller galvanometer deflection, making accurate determination of the null point more difficult. Hence, the errors of the two students are not the same.</p>
    <p><strong>Step 4: Final Conclusion</strong><br/>Therefore, errors of measurement depend on the accuracy with which \(R_2\) and \(R_1\) can be measured, and using large values of \(R_2\) and \(R_1\) makes the currents feeble, making accurate determination of the null point more difficult.</p>
